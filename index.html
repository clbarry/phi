Qualifying Exam: Paper Requirement Overview

The PHI Qualifying Exam consists of three components: Research Core Component, Health Component, and Technology Component. Each component will be assessed through a written paper designed to evaluate the candidate's knowledge and skills in the respective area. The goal of this exam is to assess a student’s aptitude for research, and to expose the student to new ideas and to faculty members who may broaden thinking about research topics in personal health informatics.

Research Core Component

Ready for Committee Review

Needs and Barriers of Healthcare Teams to Implement Real-Time AI & Predictive Analytics in Cardiothoracic ICU: A Contextual Inquiry

Carey L. Barry, Sydney K. Purdue, Mahsan Nourani, Clifton Forlines, Matthew S. Goodwin, and Melanie Tory

Abstract: Artificial Intelligence (AI) offers significant opportunities to improve healthcare quality, especially in critical care settings, where timely decisions are essential yet challenging due to the volume of continuously accumulating patient data. AI algorithms can analyze data in real time, summarize key information for busy care teams, and alert clinicians to critical events and important care milestones. However, successful implementation requires first understanding the needs and barriers: what do care teams need that is currently missing from their workflow, and what obstacles to integration exist? To address these questions, we conducted a contextual inquiry field study in a Cardiothoracic Intensive Care Unit (CT-ICU) to evaluate user needs for implementing real-time AI predictive analytics. We observed and interviewed fourteen participants (n=14), including nursing staff, advanced practice providers (NP/PAs), cardiovascular critical care attending physicians, and surgeons. Through a qualitative thematic analysis, we identified key considerations for developing and implementing static and real-time AI and predictive tools in the CT-ICU, aligning with the Technology Adoption Model (TAM) constructs of perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. Key themes included: (1) the system must have added clinical value to the clinical team and patients, (2) trust in the AI system should be prioritized through co-design processes, transparency in the system, and consistent prediction accuracy, and (3) integration into workflow was critical, including robust onboarding and minimizing alarm fatigue, charting burden, click burden, and technology burden with consideration of personal wellness. Lastly, we suggest an AI technology development framework to design for clinical team trust and adoption, Trusted AI for Clinical Teams (TACT) framework. These findings offer the potential to bridge from AI research to clinical implementation.

TRACE: Visualizing Parent-Child Physiological Concordance

Carey L. Barry, Julia Weppler, Yuna Watanabe, Anna Wallisch, Michelle A. Borkin, Matthew S. Goodwin, Melanie Tory

Abstract: Temporal Representation of Annotated-behavior and Concordance across Events (TRACE) is a visual analytics tool to help scientists and clinicians assess physiological concordance (PC) and behavioral interactions in parent-child dyads (i.e., pairs). PC is the alignment of physiological responses during a psychosocial interaction, for example, a child playing with a parent. Existing PC visualizations are limited and not easily interpretable. This design study introduces an open-source system to visualize dyadic interactions via moment-level indicators, aggregate summaries, and video navigation. Evaluation with two domain experts demonstrates that TRACE aids in interpretation of PC data, making it more accessible to non-specialists. Although the system was designed to assess dyadic parent-child interactions involving typically developing children and children with autism, the design blueprint is reusable to visualize PC in other applied contexts, including dynamics in couples or parent-child interactions around eating behaviors.
